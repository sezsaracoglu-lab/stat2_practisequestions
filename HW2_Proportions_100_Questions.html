Symptom → diagnosis → treatment

Every question type in this material. Read the left column as “what the question says”, the middle as “what it actually is”, and the right as “what you do about it”.

✅ Conditions and which method
“Is the use of the Normal approximation justified?” with a claimed p and an n
Condition check on an EXPECTED count
Multiply: np and n(1−p). Both at least 10 → yes. Never npq, never n alone.
“Should I use the plus four method or the large sample interval?”
Condition check on an OBSERVED count
Count the successes and the failures you actually saw. Either one under 10 → plus four.
A small n, or a p-hat very close to 0 or 1, and you are asked for an interval
Plus four territory
Small counts, not small n, are what break the large-sample interval. 46 of 50 has only 4 failures, so it needs plus four even though n = 50.
Two groups, and the question asks whether the Normal approximation holds
Two-sample condition check
Intervals want all four counts at least 10. Tests want at least 5 per cell. When unsure, use 10.
📈 Sampling distribution of p̂
“What is the probability that more than / less than …% of the sample …”
A probability about p-hat, not a test
There is no H₀ here. SE = √(p(1−p)/n) from the GIVEN p, then z = (p̂ − p)/SE, then one tail of the Normal table.
“The sampling distribution of p-hat is centred on … with spread …”
The three facts about a sampling distribution
Centre p, spread √(p(1−p)/n), shape approximately Normal for large n. Spread involves √n, never n.
📏 One-proportion interval
“The margin of error for a 95% confidence interval is …”
Margin of error only — not the interval
m = z* × √(p̂(1−p̂)/n). Stop there. An option written as a pair of numbers is the interval, not the margin.
“Find a 95% confidence interval for the true proportion …”
One-proportion interval, large sample
p̂ ± z*√(p̂(1−p̂)/n), with z* = 1.645 / 1.96 / 2.576 for 90 / 95 / 99%.
“The standard error for a confidence interval for … is”
SE alone, from the DATA
√(p̂(1−p̂)/n). No z*, no doubling. If the question had said “for the test”, you would use p₀ instead.
➕ Plus four
Small counts and the words “confidence interval”
Plus-four interval
p̃ = (X+2)/(n+4). Then p̃ ± z*√(p̃(1−p̃)/(n+4)). Both the estimate and the n change — never mix p̃ with the old n.
Two small groups and the words “confidence interval for the difference”
Two-sample plus four
One success and one failure into EACH group: p̃ᵢ = (Xᵢ+1)/(nᵢ+2). Then the ordinary unpooled formula on those.
🔢 How many people
“The required sample size is …” with a margin of error named in advance
Sample-size planning
n = (z*/m)² p*(1−p*). Use the stated guess for p*, or 0.5 if none is given. Always round UP, even from .1.
⚖️ One-proportion test
An H₀ with a single number in it, and “what is the value of the test statistic”
One-proportion z test
z = (p̂ − p₀) ⁄ √(p₀(1−p₀)/n). The SE uses p₀ — the null is the thing on trial, so you stand inside it.
The same setup, and “what is the P-value”
Tail area for that z
One-sided Hₐ → one tail. Two-sided Hₐ → double it. Read the direction off Hₐ, not off the data.
A test and an interval disagree about the same claim
The p₀ vs p̂ distinction
They use different standard errors, so they can disagree near the boundary. Neither is a mistake.
🔍 Reading the result
“P-value > 0.05” and a question about some other α
Not enough information
Knowing P > .05 does not tell you whether P is above or below .10. You cannot decide at a looser α from a coarser statement.
“We may conclude …” after a P-value is quoted
Definition of a P-value
The probability of data at least this extreme IF H₀ were true. Never the probability that H₀ is true, and never a claim about repeated samples of the observed difference.
A confidence interval for a difference, and “what should you conclude”
Does the interval contain 0?
Contains 0 → the two may be equivalent. Entirely above 0 → the first is bigger. Entirely below → the second is. Nothing about which is “better” beyond that.
“Not significantly different” and three tempting conclusions
Fail to reject is not proof
No evidence of a difference ≠ evidence of no difference. Say what the test can support and nothing more.
🪞 Two-proportion interval
“A 90% confidence interval for p₁ − p₂ is …”
Two-proportion interval, unpooled
(p̂₁ − p̂₂) ± z*√(p̂₁(1−p̂₁)/n₁ + p̂₂(1−p̂₂)/n₂). Each group keeps its own p̂. Variances add, standard deviations do not.
An interval for a difference, written as “0.075 ± 0.071”
Estimate ± margin, same thing
The first number is p̂₁ − p̂₂, the second is z* × unpooled SE. Check the estimate first — it rules out half the options in one subtraction.
🤝 Two-proportion test
“What null and alternative hypotheses should be established?”
Writing Hₐ from a sentence
H₀ is always equality. The direction of Hₐ follows the claim, in whatever order the question names the groups. Read the subscripts.
“What is the estimate of the overall proportion?”
The pooled proportion
p̂ = (X₁ + X₂) ⁄ (n₁ + n₂) — total successes over total people. Never the average of the two proportions.
Two groups, H₀: p₁ = p₂, and “the test statistic is …”
Two-proportion z test, pooled SE
Pool for the SE: √(p̂(1−p̂)(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)). The numerator stays p̂₁ − p̂₂, unpooled.
Two groups and “the P-value of your test is …”
Tail area, direction from Hₐ
Hₐ: p₁ ≠ p₂ → two tails. One-sided Hₐ → one tail, and the sign of z must agree with Hₐ or the P-value is above 0.5.
➗ Relative risk
“How many times as likely …” or a ratio of two proportions
Relative risk
RR = p̂₁ ⁄ p̂₂, and the null value is 1, not 0. A big ratio on two small proportions can still be a tiny difference — quote both.

Question 1

✅ Conditions and which method

The counts condition for an interval

The correct rule for proportions is that we can create confidence intervals for p based on:
  • aif n is at least 10.
  • bat least 10 successes and 10 failures.
  • cif n ≥ 30.
🔎 What it is really asking

Which condition has to hold before you are allowed to use the ordinary large-sample confidence interval for a proportion?

🧭 Which type of question this is

A pure conditions question. No arithmetic at all — it is testing whether you know that proportions are judged on COUNTS, not on sample size.

🛠️ The procedure

Count the successes you observed and the failures you observed. Both at least 10 → the large-sample interval is allowed. Either one under 10 → plus four.

🧮 The working
The large-sample interval is a Normal approximation to a binomial. The approximation depends on having enough of BOTH outcomes, not on n.

n = 1000 with 3 successes fails. The counts are 3 and 997 — the distribution of p̂ is squashed against zero and nothing like Normal, however huge n is.

n = 20 with 10 successes passes. Counts of 10 and 10, so the approximation is fine on a sample of twenty.

So the rule is: at least 10 successes AND at least 10 failures.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.n ≥ 10 is not enough on its own. With n = 40 and 2 successes the counts are 2 and 38, and the interval is unusable.
c.n ≥ 30 is the rule of thumb for MEANS, borrowed from the Central Limit Theorem. Proportions do not use it — this is the single most common crossover mistake.
🏷️ For proportions, count outcomes. n ≥ 30 belongs to means and nowhere near this course’s proportion work.

Question 2

✅ Conditions and which method

Choosing plus four from the counts

In a sample of 20 items, I found six to be defective. In constructing a confidence interval for the proportion of defectives, I should use:
  • athe plus four method.
  • bthe large sample CI interval.
  • cneither method.
🔎 What it is really asking

Apply question 1’s rule to actual numbers. Count the successes and the failures and see whether both clear 10.

🧭 Which type of question this is

The same conditions question, now with data. This is the decision you make BEFORE any formula.

🛠️ The procedure

Successes = the thing you are estimating the proportion of. Failures = the rest. Both ≥ 10 → large sample. Either < 10 → plus four.

🧮 The working
The proportion being estimated is the proportion defective, so defectives are the successes.

Successes = 6
Failures = 20 − 6 = 14

14 is fine. 6 is not. One failed count is enough to rule out the large-sample interval.

So: plus four. p̃ = (6 + 2) ⁄ (20 + 4) = 8 ⁄ 24 = 0.3333, and the interval uses n = 24.
✗ Why the others are wrong
b.The large-sample interval needs both counts at least 10. With only 6 successes it is not available — this is the option that catches people who check n (20 ≥ 10) instead of the counts.
c.For an INTERVAL, “neither” is never the answer: plus four exists precisely so that small counts still have a usable one. (A two-proportion TEST can genuinely be unavailable — see Exam C, question 23 — but that is a different procedure.)
🏷️ Plus four is not a last resort — it is the standard fix for an interval. Small counts, not small n, is what triggers it.

Question 3

📏 One-proportion interval

Margin of error for one proportion

A sample of 75 students found that 55 of them had cell phones. The margin of error for a 95% confidence interval estimate for the proportion of all students with cell phones is:
  • a0.084.
  • b(0.633, 0.833).
  • c0.100.
  • d0.093
🔎 What it is really asking

Just the ± part. Not the interval, not the standard error — the number you would add and subtract.

🧭 Which type of question this is

One-proportion margin of error. The giveaway is that three of the four options are single numbers: a margin is one number, an interval is two.

🛠️ The procedure

m = z* × √(p̂(1−p̂)/n). Find p̂, find the SE, multiply by the z* for the stated confidence level.

🧮 The working
p̂ = 55 ⁄ 75 = 0.7333

Counts: successes 55, failures 20 — both over 10, so the large-sample formula is allowed.

SE = √(0.7333 × 0.2667 ⁄ 75) = 0.0511

95% → z* = 1.96

m = 1.96 × 0.0511 = 0.1001, which rounds to 0.100.

(For your own check, the interval itself would be (0.633, 0.833) — that is option b, and it is not what was asked.)
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.0.084 is the margin at 90% confidence: 1.645 × 0.0511 = 0.0840. The right formula with the wrong z*.
b.That is the confidence interval, not the margin of error. Reading the question is half the mark here.
d.0.093 is a near miss with no correct route to it. It exists so that “about 0.1” is not good enough — you have to compute and then look.
🏷️ Margin = one number. Interval = two numbers. Standard error = the margin with the z* taken off.

Question 4

✅ Conditions and which method

The Normal approximation on an expected count

It is known that 12% of all students taking a particular course receive a grade of A. There are 155 students in one section of the course. Is the use of the Normal distribution approximation justified in this case?
  • aYes, because np > 10 and nq > 10
  • bYes, because np > 10 and npq > 10
  • cNo, because np > 10 and nq > 10
  • dNo, because np > 10 and npq > 10
🔎 What it is really asking

Does the Normal approximation hold here — and, just as importantly, which two products do you check?

🧭 Which type of question this is

Condition check on an expected count. The population proportion is given (12%), so you multiply rather than count.

🛠️ The procedure

np and n(1−p), both at least 10. Note that this is nq, not npq — npq is the variance of the count and has nothing to do with the condition.

🧮 The working
p = 0.12, so q = 1 − p = 0.88, and n = 155.

np = 155 × 0.12 = 18.6 ✓ (over 10)
nq = 155 × 0.88 = 136.4 ✓ (over 10)

Both clear 10, so the answer is yes, and the reason is np > 10 and nq > 10.

For contrast: npq = 155 × 0.12 × 0.88 = 16.37. That is the variance of the number of A grades. It is a real quantity — it is just not the condition.
✗ Why the others are wrong
b.npq is the variance of the count, not a condition. The pair you check is always np and n(1−p).
c.The reason given is correct, but the verdict is not. If both products clear 10, the answer is yes.
d.Wrong verdict and wrong condition. This option is only tempting if you are skimming for the letters “npq”.
🏷️ np and nq, both over 10. Say it as “enough expected successes and enough expected failures”. npq is a different animal.

Question 5

📈 Sampling distribution of p̂

A probability about p̂

It is known that 12% of all students taking a particular course receive a grade of A. There are 155 students in one section of the course. What is the probability that at least 14% of them receive a grade of A?
  • a0.7794
  • b0.1103
  • c0.2206
  • d0.5223
🔎 What it is really asking

If the true rate really is 12%, how often would a class of 155 throw up 14% or more A grades just by chance?

🧭 Which type of question this is

A probability about the sampling distribution of p̂. There is no null hypothesis and no test here — the population proportion is handed to you as a fact.

🛠️ The procedure

SE = √(p(1−p)/n) using the GIVEN p. Then z = (p̂ − p) ⁄ SE. Then one tail of the Normal table, in the direction the question asks.

🧮 The working
Centre: p = 0.12 (the sampling distribution of p̂ sits on the true p).

SE = √(0.12 × 0.88 ⁄ 155) = 0.0261

z = (0.14 − 0.12) ⁄ 0.0261 = 0.766

“At least 14%” is the upper tail:
P(Z ≥ 0.77) = 0.2218 ≈ 0.2206

In words: a 14% A-rate in a class this size is unremarkable — it would happen about one year in five with no change in the course at all.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.0.7794 is the other tail: 1 − 0.2206. It answers “at most 14%”. Sketch the curve and shade before you read the table.
b.0.1103 is exactly half of 0.2206 — the mistake of halving a tail that was already one-sided.
d.0.5223 has no correct route to it at all — it needs a z of about 0.06. It sits just over a half, which is where you land whenever z comes out near zero, so it catches an SE that has gone badly wrong somewhere. The defence is to sanity-check z before you reach for the table.
🏷️ A question with no H₀ in it is a sampling-distribution question. Use the p you were given, not a p̂ from data.

Question 6

📏 One-proportion interval

One-proportion interval, large sample

You buy a package of 122 Smarties and 19 of them are red. What is a 95% confidence interval for the true proportion of red Smarties?
  • a(0.092, 0.220)
  • b(0.103, 0.230)
  • c(0.085, 0.199)
  • d(0.087, 0.220)
🔎 What it is really asking

Estimate the true red rate, with a 95% interval around it.

🧭 Which type of question this is

Straight one-proportion interval. First decide large-sample or plus four, then compute.

🛠️ The procedure

Check the counts. If both ≥ 10: p̂ ± 1.96 √(p̂(1−p̂)/n).

🧮 The working
Counts: successes 19, failures 103 — both over 10, so large sample.

p̂ = 19 ⁄ 122 = 0.1557

SE = √(0.1557 × 0.8443 ⁄ 122) = 0.0328

m = 1.96 × 0.0328 = 0.0643

Interval = 0.1557 ± 0.0643 = (0.091, 0.220) ≈ (0.092, 0.220)

Check the centre before anything else: the midpoint of the right answer has to be 0.156. The midpoints on offer are 0.156, 0.167, 0.142 and 0.154 — so that one check kills three of the four options before you compute anything.
✗ Why the others are wrong
b.That is roughly the plus-four interval: p̃ = 21 ⁄ 126 = 0.1667 gives (0.102, 0.232). Right formula, wrong method — the counts here are 19 and 103, so plus four is not needed.
c.Centred on about 0.142, which is not p̂. An interval has to sit symmetrically on your estimate.
d.The upper limit is right and the lower one is not — and p̂ ± m cannot produce a lopsided interval. One of the two numbers must be wrong.
🏷️ Before computing anything, find p̂ and look at the midpoints on offer. Most wrong intervals are not even centred in the right place.

Question 7

🔢 How many people

Sample size for a given margin

We want to construct a 95% confidence interval for the true proportion of all adult males who have spent time in prison, with a margin of error of 0.02. From previous studies, we believe the proportion to be somewhere around 0.07. The required sample size is therefore:
  • a620.
  • b626.
  • c632.
  • d635
🔎 What it is really asking

How many people do you need to recruit so that the ± on your interval comes out no wider than 0.02?

🧭 Which type of question this is

Sample-size planning. The telltale signs: the margin of error is named in advance, and no data exist yet.

🛠️ The procedure

n = (z*/m)² × p*(1−p*). Use the prior guess for p* when one is offered, 0.5 when it is not. Then round UP, always.

🧮 The working
z* = 1.96, m = 0.02, p* = 0.07

(z* ⁄ m)² = (1.96 ⁄ 0.02)² = 98² = 9604

p*(1 − p*) = 0.07 × 0.93 = 0.0651

n = 9604 × 0.0651 = 625.22

Round up: n = 626

Rounding up is not fussiness. 625 people would leave the margin a hair above 0.02, which is exactly what you promised not to do.

Worth noticing: with no prior guess you would use p* = 0.5 and need 2401 — nearly four times as many. A believable prior is worth a great deal of money.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.620 has no correct route to it — rounding 625.2 the wrong way gives 625, and rounding to the nearest ten gives 630. It is simply a tidy-looking number parked near the answer.
c.632 cannot be reached by any correct route. It is here so that “roughly 625” does not earn the mark.
d.635 likewise. The four options are deliberately close so that you have to compute rather than estimate.
🏷️ Always round up — even from .1. You cannot interview 625.2 people, and 625 breaks your promise.

Question 8

⚖️ One-proportion test

One-proportion test statistic

⚠ the printed key and the arithmetic disagree — read the note
In a sample of 446 students, 246 ate breakfast. Can we conclude that more than 50% of all students eat breakfast?

We will test H₀: p = 0.50   Hₐ: p > 0.50.

What is the value of the test statistic?
  • a2.11
  • b5.62
  • c2.31
  • d4.85
🔎 What it is really asking

How many standard errors above 0.50 does the observed 55% sit, measured on the ruler the null hypothesis provides?

🧭 Which type of question this is

One-proportion z test, statistic only. The signature is an H₀ with a single number in it.

🛠️ The procedure

z = (p̂ − p₀) ⁄ √(p₀(1−p₀)/n). The standard error uses p₀, not p̂ — you are standing inside the null hypothesis and asking how odd the data look from there.

🧮 The working
p̂ = 246 ⁄ 446 = 0.5516

p₀ = 0.50, so the null’s SE is:
SE = √(0.50 × 0.50 ⁄ 446) = √(0.25 ⁄ 446) = 0.0237

z = (0.5516 − 0.50) ⁄ 0.0237 = 0.0516 ⁄ 0.0237 = 2.18

The official key is a, 2.11, and of the four options that is the closest to the computed value — see the red note below.

Either way, the shape of the answer is the point: just over two standard errors above the null, which is enough to reject at 5% and not at 1%.
✗ Why the others are wrong
b.5.62 is far too large to come from this formula. A z this size on n = 446 would mean p̂ was about 0.63.
c.2.31 is the other plausible-looking option, and it is the one to beware of: it is close enough that you cannot choose between 2.11 and 2.31 by feel. Compute.
d.4.85 is, like 5.62, a decoy well outside the reachable range.
⚠ THE PRINTED KEY AND THE ARITHMETIC DISAGREE — READ THE NOTE

Working this from scratch gives z = 2.1782, not 2.11.

p̂ = 246⁄446 = 0.551570, SE = 0.023676, so z = 2.1782 — and the matching one-sided P-value is 0.0147, not the 0.0174 that question 9 keys.

Of the four options offered, 2.11 is the nearest (0.07 away, against 0.13 for 2.31), and 2.11 and 0.0174 are consistent with each other — so the official key is self-consistent and is kept here exactly as printed. And the route to it is identifiable: round p̂ to 0.55 before dividing, and (0.55 − 0.50) ⁄ 0.023676 = 2.1119 → 2.11, whose one-sided tail is 0.0174 — exactly the key to question 9. So the source rounded p̂ early, which is the one habit this page keeps telling you not to copy.

What to do in the exam: compute z, then pick the closest option. If your z comes out at 2.18 and the options say 2.11 and 2.31, choose 2.11 and move on.

🏷️ Test SE uses p₀. Interval SE uses p̂. This is the single most examined distinction in the whole lecture.

Question 9

⚖️ One-proportion test

P-value for a one-sided test

In a sample of 446 students, 246 ate breakfast. Can we conclude that more than 50% of all students eat breakfast?

We will test H₀: p = 0.50 vs. Hₐ: p > 0.50.

What is the P-value of the test?
  • a0.0174
  • b0.1765
  • c0.2876
  • d0.0896
🔎 What it is really asking

If exactly half of all students ate breakfast, how often would a sample of 446 show 55% or more?

🧭 Which type of question this is

The tail area for the z you just computed. One question, two marks — this pattern (statistic, then P-value) is everywhere in this paper.

🛠️ The procedure

Look up the z. Hₐ has a single direction (p > 0.50), so take ONE tail, on the side Hₐ points to. Do not double it.

🧮 The working
From question 8, z ≈ 2.11 (the key’s value).

Hₐ: p > 0.50, so the P-value is the upper tail:
P(Z ≥ 2.11) = 0.0174 ≈ 0.0174

Decision: 0.0174 < 0.05, so reject H₀. There is evidence that more than half of all students eat breakfast.

But 0.0174 > 0.01, so you could not reject at the 1% level. Always say which α you are using.

If you had doubled the tail by habit you would get 0.0349 — not on the list, which is the exam telling you the test was one-sided.
✗ Why the others are wrong
b.0.1765 is the tail for a z near 0.93 — nowhere near 2.11. It catches a misread table row.
c.0.2876 is the tail for a z near 0.56. Same mistake, further off.

Question 24

🤝 Two-proportion test

Writing the hypotheses from a claim

A nutritionist wants to determine if males consume more calcium than females. Of the 180 males in her sample, 99 indicated they consumed the recommended daily intake of calcium, compared with 232 of the 320 females.

The nutritionist wants to test whether the proportions of males and females that consume the recommended daily intake of calcium are the same. What null and alternative hypotheses should the nutritionist establish?
  • aH₀: p_f = p_m vs. Hₐ: p_f &gt; p_m
  • bH₀: p_f = p_m vs. Hₐ: p_f &lt; p_m
  • cH₀: p_f &lt; p_m vs. Hₐ: p_f = p_m
🔎 What it is really asking

Turn “males consume more than females” into symbols — with the subscripts in the order the options use.

🧭 Which type of question this is

Hypothesis writing. No arithmetic, and the trap is entirely in the subscripts.

🛠️ The procedure

H₀ is always the equality. Then translate the claim, and check it against the order of the subscripts in front of you — here the options put females first.

🧮 The working
The claim: males consume more than females, i.e. p_m &gt; p_f.

Now rewrite it with females on the left, because that is how the options are written:
p_m > p_f is the same statement as p_f &lt; p_m.

So: H₀: p_f = p_m vs. Hₐ: p_f &lt; p_m

Two rules worth keeping:
• H₀ always carries the equals sign. A hypothesis pair with an inequality in H₀ is malformed and can be eliminated on sight.
• The direction of Hₐ comes from the claim, never from the data. The data here actually go the other way (55% of males against 72.5% of females), and that does not change the hypotheses one bit.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.This says females are higher, which reverses the claim. It is the option you pick if you translate “males more” into “left side bigger” without checking which group is on the left.
c.H₀ cannot contain an inequality, and Hₐ cannot be the equality. This one is eliminable without reading the scenario at all.
🏷️ Write the claim your own way first, then rotate it to match the options’ subscript order. Never let the data choose the direction of Hₐ.

A nutritionist wants to determine if males consume more calcium than females. Of the 180 males in her sample, 99 indicated they consumed the recommended daily intake of calcium, compared to 232 of the 320 females.

What is the absolute value of the test statistic?
  • a8.27
  • b8.14
  • c3.97
  • d3.65

Compute z for the two groups and report it without the sign.

🧭 Which type of question this is

Two-proportion z test, same procedure as questions 19, 22 and 23 — and the distractors are built from one specific mistake.

🛠️ The procedure

Pooled SE, and the pooling must keep both sample sizes: the (1/n₁ + 1/n₂) factor is what makes it a two-sample standard error.

🧮 The working
p̂_m = 99 ⁄ 180 = 0.550
p̂_f = 232 ⁄ 320 = 0.725

Difference = 0.550 − 0.725 = -0.175

Pooled p̂ = (99 + 232) ⁄ (180 + 320) = 331 ⁄ 500 = 0.662

SE = √(0.662 × 0.338 × (1⁄180 + 1⁄320))
   = √(0.2238 × 0.008681) = 0.0441

z = -0.175 ⁄ 0.0441 = -3.97, so |z| = 3.97 ≈ 3.97

d.3.65 is the near miss to the right answer. The unpooled SE would give -3.92, still not 3.65 — so this one is simply arithmetic drift.
🏷️ A two-sample SE must contain both n’s. If your z suddenly doubles, you have lost the (1/n₁ + 1/n₂).

A study reports P = 0.08 for a comparison of two proportions. Which statement is correct?
  • aThere is an 8% probability that the null hypothesis of equal proportions is true.
  • bThe difference actually observed would be obtained in about 8% of repeated samples of this size.

Check each candidate for both halves. A sentence missing either one is wrong.

🧮 The working
The correct sentence has both parts:

• “at least as large as the one observed” — the extremeness half ✓
• “if the two proportions were truly equal” — the given-H₀ half ✓

c.Same inversion as above, dressed as a complement. 1 − P is not the probability of Hₐ.

  • b0.0077
  • c0.0153
  • d0.4300
🔎 What it is really asking

The data point the opposite way from the claim. What does the P-value do?

🧭 Which type of question this is

One-proportion test where the sign of z disagrees with Hₐ — the situation most people fumble.

🛠️ The procedure

Compute z as usual, then take the tail Hₐ points to, even though the data are on the other side. The P-value will come out above 0.5, and that is correct.

🧮 The working
p̂ = 129 ⁄ 300 = 0.43 — already below 0.50, so the data contradict Hₐ before you start.

SE = √(0.50 × 0.50 ⁄ 300) = 0.0289

z = (0.43 − 0.50) ⁄ 0.0289 = -2.42

Hₐ: p > 0.50 → the upper tail, regardless of where the data fell:
P = P(Z ≥ -2.42) = 0.9923

b.0.0077 is the lower tail. It would be the P-value for Hₐ: p < 0.50 — a different alternative, chosen after seeing the data, which is not allowed.
c.0.0153 doubles the lower tail: wrong tail and wrong number of tails.
d.0.4300 is p̂, not a probability about anything.
🏷️ Hₐ picks the tail before you look at the data. A P-value above 0.5 is a legitimate result, not a mistake.

The sample-size calculation without the z*. Watch for that: no confidence level is mentioned.

All three steps: the statistic, the P-value, and the decision.

🛠️ The procedure

Pooled SE → z → one tail (Hₐ is directional) → compare with 0.05.

🧮 The working
Pooled p̂ = 0.5862

SE = √(0.5862 × 0.4138 × (1⁄420 + 1⁄160)) = 0.0458

z = (0.600 − 0.550) ⁄ 0.0458 = 1.09

🔎 What it is really asking

Compute both the ratio and the difference, and be clear about which is which.

🧭 Which type of question this is

Relative risk. The null value is 1, not 0, and the ratio and difference tell different stories.

🛠️ The procedure

RR = p̂₁ ⁄ p̂₂. Difference = p̂₁ − p̂₂. Report both: a ratio alone can make a tiny difference sound enormous.

🧮 The working
p̂₁ = 42 ⁄ 350 = 0.12 (12%)
p̂₂ = 24 ⁄ 400 = 0.06 (6%)

Relative risk = 0.12 ⁄ 0.06 = 2.00 — group 1’s risk is twice group 2’s.

Absolute difference = 0.12 − 0.06 = 0.06 — six percentage points.

Both are true and they sound very different. “Twice the risk” and “six more cases per hundred” describe the same two numbers.

a.0.50 is p̂₂ ⁄ p̂₁ — the ratio the other way up. Relative risk is sensitive to which group you call 1, so say which.
b.1.06 adds the 0.06 difference to 1. A ratio is not one plus a difference; those are two separate quantities.
c.The relative risk is right, and “twice as serious” is not a statistical statement. Twice as likely says nothing about severity.
